Most research on clientelism treats parties as active buyers and voters as passive targets. We argue that this misses vote selling, where voters initiate transactions and price their support. Building on prospect theory, we develop a formal model that conceptualizes vote buying and vote selling as distinct institutional arrangements that shift bargaining initiative, perceived electoral losses, and surplus allocation. When parties initiate, loss-averse parties insure against defeat by targeting ideologically proximate voters and may overpay to avoid electoral losses. When voters initiate, they request higher prices from parties with greater effective willingness to pay, especially electorally strong parties, but face rejection risk. A laboratory experiment varying initiative, ideology, electoral strength, and pivotality supports these expectations. Party-initiated vote buying concentrates resources among core supporters and generates higher voter payoffs. Voter-initiated vote selling shifts requested prices toward electorally advantaged parties but does not necessarily increase voter surplus.
